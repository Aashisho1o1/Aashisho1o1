RipplX, an AI research assistant for SEC filings

RipplX reads a company's 10-K and other SEC filings against the prior period and writes a short brief of what materially changed, the reading an analyst or investor would otherwise repeat every filing season. It also watches for shifts in tone in the risk sections and for patterns such as revenue growing faster than the cash coming in.

the model
Chooses which findings matter, writes the headline, and judges how important each one is.
the code
Checks that every quote exists at the exact filing, section, and character offset, and that every number matches what the company filed. The model never does arithmetic.
on failure
A bad finding is dropped on its own. A provider error or missing coverage holds back the whole brief.

An optional skeptic model can object to a finding but can never approve one. I built it that way because someone putting money behind a conclusion should be able to see where it came from, and in finance a confident wrong sentence costs more than a missing one. The checks prove the quotes and the numbers, not that the model read them correctly, so every result in the current alpha also gets a human review.

Next is brokerage connections, starting with Robinhood, so an investor sees how a filing change bears on the holdings they already own rather than on one stock in isolation. I'm also building a research agent that, when someone is weighing a purchase, assembles a cited analysis of the company, its industry, and its key financial metrics, the first draft an associate would otherwise have to put together.

Traume, a trauma-informed support chatbot

A prototype for people in emotional distress, where one careless sentence does real harm. Crisis checks run before the model is ever called, and a crisis gets emergency resources right away. The model then works under a prompt that rules out victim blaming, minimizing, and legal or medical directives, and its reply passes a second set of rules before anyone sees it. Anything unsafe or unreadable is replaced with a safe fallback.

Conversations are not stored on the server, and there are no cookies or analytics. An adversarial test suite covers victim blaming, overpromised confidentiality, suggestions to contact an abuser, and missed or false crisis alarms, and the run fails if the rule-based layer drops below 90 percent.

Alumni Disaster Tracker

A service that reads NASA's live event feed, measures the distance from each active wildfire, earthquake, or storm to alumni addresses, and lets the alumni office reach anyone near the affected area the same day. I tested it with office staff who had never used a mapping interface and redesigned the filters and labels around how they actually worked.

In the public rebuild, the proximity check for 2,000 alumni went from 15.5 seconds to about 10 milliseconds by computing every distance in one NumPy operation. The demo uses 200 synthetic alumni, because a public demo should never expose where real people live.

BlueHabit

A route planner for wheelchair users and people with vision loss, built over a weekend with three teammates. It placed fourth out of more than 30 teams. The shortest route is often the wrong answer for someone in a wheelchair, so BlueHabit combines air quality and satellite data to suggest routes that are actually walkable.

The analysis was the easier half. The harder question was what a person who has never used a mapping tool needs to see in the result, and what to leave out.
